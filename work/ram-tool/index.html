Ram Tool, now White Cap

A stalled ecommerce redesign got back on track fairly quickly and shipped on time, and the client asked me back for innovation work.

Took over the client conversations and reframed the team's work around users, data and outcomes.Senior UX Lead · Razorfish, now Publicis Sapient

Problem

Ram Tool was a construction supply distributor in Birmingham, Alabama, since acquired by White Cap, and our team at Razorfish was redesigning its ecommerce site. When I stepped in, the project was stalling. The lead designer was showing the client a single option and pushing back when they asked for changes, and the client was understandably frustrated.

The deeper problem was framing. The team was fairly inexperienced, and nobody had started from the people using the site. No one had asked about personas, the data the client already had, or the outcomes the business needed.

Outcome

  • The project got back on track fairly quickly, and we delivered on time.
  • The client invited me back for innovation exercises.
  • I don't have post-launch numbers for this one. The win I can speak to is a client relationship that went from frustrated to asking for more.

Approach

I became the design team's face to the client. Mostly that meant asking the questions that hadn't been asked yet: who buys from Ram Tool, what their data already said, and what a better site needed to change for the business.

Inside the team, I worked with design and product to build the work on those answers. Instead of one design to defend, the team brought several options, each with a reason behind it. That way the client could weigh in on the thinking, not just the look.

The work

What I learned

This project confirmed something I'd learned over years of client work: whoever is ultimately responsible for the work has to feel they own it. When a team shows a single option, clients often feel left out of the process, and that's about where Ram Tool was. So I bring several options, each with a reason behind it, and usually one I know will be polarizing. Sometimes that's the one they pick, because it sparks something. More often it's too far from the direction, but it still does two things: it gives the client a real choice, and it moves us toward a direction, which keeps the work on track.

My role

I led the relationship with the client and set the direction for how the team framed and presented its work. To get things back on track, I gave the team heavier direction than I normally would. I wireframed some of the key pages to give the designers a starting point and wrote some of the copy myself to fill in gaps, but the finished pages were the team's design.
