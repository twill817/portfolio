Who it was for

We built 10 months of research, with 566 residents, around three life events. These are the people behind them:

  • The first-time applicant

    Just lost their income and starts by searching. That’s why search, with life-event filters, became a main way in.

  • The caregiver

    Managing care for themselves or an aging parent. Half of those we tested couldn’t tell what “ECM” meant, so we renamed labels for what residents look for.

  • The small business owner

    Starting something like a frozen-treat shop, across five state sites. That’s why I recommended bringing business tasks into one place.

We also recruited screen reader users and residents living with cognitive and learning disabilities, because a state site has to work for all of them.

Problem

Pennsylvania's services were spread across 62 sites run by 43 agencies. Residents had to know which agency owned their task before they could find it, which was the opposite of the governor's "No wrong door" commitment.

Approach

We worked with each agency to reorganize its site around how residents look for content, not how the agency is set up. Folders gave way to tags, so every page carries its agency, topic, and life events, and search can filter the way residents think.

The hard call

Early on, we planned to build the experience around life events, but no one on the client side would own that content. So we kept the recommendations simpler and put the effort into examples and guidance agencies could follow on their own.

The people we worked with at the agencies were mostly site managers, not designers or writers. They knew their sites had grown messy over time, so they welcomed a better approach, and once they had the pattern, they didn't have to think about it. We backed that up with a content authoring guidelines site and training on AEM, the new components, and content styling.

Research

Before talking to residents, we looked at how other governments had solved this: USA.gov, GOV.UK, and several other states. USA.gov organizes content around life events, which shaped our original direction, and GOV.UK was the best organized of the group.

We ran the research across three life events: a financial shock, health and aging, and starting a business. That included interviews, card sorts, tree tests, and prototype tests, and we recruited screen reader users and residents living with cognitive and learning disabilities.

Words residents actually use

The tree tests showed the structure mostly worked, but the words didn't. Half of the patients and caregivers we tested couldn't tell what "ECM" meant, and more than half were lost on "OCDEL Announcements." I worked through the results with our researcher and made the final call on the labels, and the rule was simple: name the thing a resident is looking for, not the program or office that owns it.

The work

Business in PA

The governor wanted a one-stop shop for business owners. What they had was five separate sites with different menus and different words for the same things, including a checklist that asked a frozen-treat shop whether it sells tires. I reviewed all five and recommended bringing the tasks into one place.

I designed the concepts we tested with 12 owners: a guide that tailors itself after a few questions, a dashboard for deadlines and permits, and a home for documents. One owner said it seemed like the state really wanted her to succeed. The redesign didn’t move forward while I was on the project.

What I learned

Looking back, the ceiling was set from the start. The budget and deadline were firm, and the governor's office wanted a clearer, better-organized site, then to move on. We pitched more, including the wizard, but none of it fit the scope. I think that's sometimes the job: do the agreed work well, show what's possible next, and let the client decide.

With today's AI tools, I'd also take the content team's crawler site maps further, looking for patterns our pragmatic pass missed, and give agencies a way to test how a change affects the path to their content.

My role

I owned the UX architecture and led a UX team of four, including another associate creative director who led visual design, working directly with the governor's digital team. I ran the agency workshops, set the content strategy behind search, and made the final call on labels and structure from the tree test results.

I oversaw the design system too. The visual design wasn't mine, but I reviewed the team's work, worked through accessibility issues with the accessibility lead, and made sure the intent stayed clear.

I also built the user types and journey maps the team worked from. The faces on the user types are images I made with Midjourney, an early use of AI in my client work.
