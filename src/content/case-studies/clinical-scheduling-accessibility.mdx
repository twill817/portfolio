## The problem

Meridian's internal scheduling tool — used by front-desk and clinical staff to book, move, and manage patient appointments across 40 clinics — failed a third-party accessibility audit against WCAG 2.1 AA. Beyond the compliance risk, we had staff members with low vision and motor impairments who were working around a tool that wasn't built for them, using workarounds that put patient scheduling accuracy at risk.

The naive fix — bolt on ARIA labels and call it done — would have technically passed an audit while doing nothing for how the tool actually felt to use under pressure, mid-shift, with a line of patients at the desk.

## My role

I led the accessibility overhaul as the principal designer on the internal tools team, working with one other designer, two engineers, and an external accessibility consultant for audit and validation.

## Process

**Test with the people who actually hit the problem.** We recruited five clinic staff members who used assistive technology day-to-day — screen readers, switch devices, screen magnification — and ran the entire redesign process around their real workflows, not simulated ones.

**Redesign the interaction model, not just the markup.** The existing scheduling grid was a dense, mouse-only drag-and-drop calendar. We rebuilt the core booking flow as a keyboard-operable, screen-reader-friendly step sequence that was, if anything, faster for everyone: fewer mis-clicks, clearer confirmation states, less chance of double-booking a room.

**Make speed and accessibility the same metric.** Front-desk staff are timed, informally and formally, on how fast they can book an appointment during a rush. We used that same task-completion-time metric to validate every accessibility change — a change that helped compliance but slowed down booking wasn't considered a win.

## Outcome

The rebuilt scheduling flow passed the follow-up WCAG 2.1 AA audit with zero critical issues. Average appointment-booking time dropped 18% for all staff, not just those using assistive technology. The five staff members who piloted the redesign became internal champions, training other clinics on the new flow ahead of the full rollout.
